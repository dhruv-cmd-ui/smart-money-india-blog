Direct vs Regular Mutual Funds: How Much Does It Really Cost You?

2026-10-30 · 4 min read

When you invest in a mutual fund in India, you are offered two routes for the exact same portfolio: a Regular plan or a Direct plan. Both share the same fund manager, the same underlying stocks or bonds, and the same investment objective. Yet, over 15 or 20 years, the direct plan can leave you with tens of lakhs of rupees more in your account. The difference boils down to a single mechanism: distributor commissions baked into the ongoing costs of your fund.

How the Two Plans Actually Differ

Every mutual fund scheme charges an annual percentage to manage your money, known as the Total Expense Ratio (TER). Under SEBI regulations introduced in January 2013, asset management companies must offer direct plans alongside regular plans.

In a regular plan, you invest through an intermediary—such as a bank, a broker, or an individual financial distributor. The fund house pays this intermediary an ongoing trail commission out of your investment for as long as you stay invested. This commission is added directly to the fund's expense ratio.

In a direct plan, you bypass intermediaries and invest directly with the fund house or through an execution-only platform. Because the fund house pays no distributor commissions, the TER is lower. Typically, for actively managed equity mutual funds in India, direct plans have an expense ratio that is roughly 0.5% to 1.0% lower per year than their regular counterparts. While one percent may sound negligible on day one, compounding turns it into an enormous sum over decades.

The Maths: Quantifying the Cost Over Time

To understand the real impact, consider a practical example. Suppose an investor starts a monthly Systematic Investment Plan (SIP) of ₹10,000 in an equity fund and continues it for 25 years.

  • Total amount invested: ₹10,000 × 12 months × 25 years = ₹30,00,000 (30 lakh)

Assume the underlying portfolio delivers a gross return of 13% per annum before expenses:

  • Regular plan (assuming a net return of 11.5% after a 1.5% TER):

The accumulated corpus after 25 years comes to approximately ₹1.68 crore (₹1,68,00,000).

  • Direct plan (assuming a net return of 12.5% after a 0.5% TER, a 1% difference):

The accumulated corpus after 25 years comes to approximately ₹1.99 crore (₹1,99,00,000).

The difference between the two outcomes is ₹31 lakh (₹1.99 crore minus ₹1.68 crore). By opting for the regular plan, you surrender an amount greater than your entire principal investment of ₹30 lakh over that 25-year period. You lose not only the 1% fee each year, but also all the future compounding that those deducted fees would have generated.

Does Anyone Still Need a Regular Plan?

Paying a commission is not inherently wrong if you are receiving tangible, actionable value in return. A regular plan can make sense for an investor who lacks the time, knowledge, or emotional discipline to manage their portfolio independently. A competent mutual fund distributor helps with fund selection, rebalancing, documentation, and—most importantly—preventing panic selling during market downturns.

However, many retail investors purchase regular plans unwittingly through bank relationship managers who offer no ongoing advice after the initial sale. In that scenario, paying an ongoing trail commission provides zero benefit. If you require professional financial guidance, an alternative model is to consult a SEBI-registered Investment Adviser (RIA) who charges a transparent, flat advisory fee while recommending direct mutual fund plans.

How to Check and Switch Your Existing Funds

You can easily identify which plan you hold. Look at your Consolidated Account Statement (CAS) or fund account statement. If the fund name contains the word "Direct", you are in a direct plan. If it says "Regular", or mentions no plan type at all, you are in a regular plan.

Switching from a regular plan to a direct plan is treated by tax laws as a redemption (sale) and a fresh purchase. Before you switch:

  • Check for exit loads: Most equity funds levy an exit load of around 1% if units are redeemed within 365 days of purchase.
  • Check capital gains tax: Gains realised during the switch will be subject to short-term or long-term capital gains tax under current income tax rules.

A practical approach is to stop your ongoing SIPs in regular plans, redirect all new SIPs into direct plans immediately, and systematically switch your older accumulated units once they cross the exit load and tax-efficient thresholds.

The choice between direct and regular plans is not about saving a minor transaction fee today. It is about deciding who keeps a significant share of your long-term wealth—you, or an intermediary. If you are comfortable selecting and monitoring your own funds, switching to direct plans is one of the most reliable ways to boost your portfolio's terminal value without taking on any additional market risk.
