Physical Gold vs Gold ETF vs Sovereign Gold Bonds: Comparison

2026-10-28 · 5 min read

Gold holds a central place in Indian households, serving both as a cultural staple and a traditional hedge against economic uncertainty. For decades, buying physical metal was the default choice. Today, Indian investors can also access gold through modern financial instruments like exchange-traded funds and government-backed bonds. Choosing the right route depends on your investment goal, your holding period, and how you weigh practical factors like storage and liquidity.

The Core Features: Physical, ETFs, and Bonds

- Physical gold: This includes jewellery, coins, and minted bars bought from local jewellers or banks. You hold the tangible asset in hand, but the purchase often includes making charges (ranging from a few percent up to 20% or more on jewellery) and Goods and Services Tax (GST) on purchase. - Gold ETFs (Exchange Traded Funds): Regulated by SEBI, these open-ended mutual fund schemes trade on the stock exchanges (NSE and BSE). Each unit represents physical gold of 99.5% purity held in professional vaults by custodians. Investors can buy and sell units through a standard demat and trading account, usually reflecting the market price of gold in small fractional units, such as 0.01 gram or 1 gram. - Sovereign Gold Bonds (SGBs): Issued by the Reserve Bank of India on behalf of the Government of India, SGBs represent gold in paper or demat form. Each bond unit tracks the price of one gram of gold. Original tenures run for 8 years, paying a fixed annual interest rate of 2.5% on the initial issue price, credited semi-annually to the investor's bank account. Notably, the government has not issued new primary tranches since early 2024. Therefore, investors currently acquire SGBs primarily by buying previously issued series trading on the secondary market on stock exchanges. Check current availability and trading volumes before placing an order.

Liquidity and Safety: The Practical Differences

The day-to-day handling of these assets presents stark contrasts in safety, storage, and how easily you can convert them to cash.

With physical gold, safety is your responsibility. Storing it at home brings theft risk, while keeping it in a bank locker adds annual locker rental charges. When it comes to liquidity, selling jewellery often involves melting deductions, purity verification discounts, or jeweller-specific buyback terms, meaning you rarely recover the full prevailing market value of your initial outlay.

Gold ETFs eliminate storage and purity concerns because the underlying bullion rests in institutional vaults, covered by an annual expense ratio deducted from the fund's Net Asset Value (NAV). Liquidity is high during market hours; you can sell your units on the exchange with the click of a button and receive the proceeds according to standard equity settlement cycles.

SGBs carry zero default risk regarding principal and interest payments because they are backed by the sovereign. In demat form, there is no storage cost or risk of physical theft. However, liquidity requires careful handling. While you can sell SGBs on the secondary market before their 8-year maturity, trading volumes across different series can be low. This means an investor looking to sell in a hurry might have to accept a discount to the spot price of gold.

Returns and the Impact of Yield

Gold itself does not produce income—its returns depend entirely on price appreciation. However, how you own the gold alters your net return.

Suppose you invest ₹1,00,000 in an SGB trading at par on the exchange, with 5 years left until maturity. Over these 5 years, the 2.5% fixed annual interest generates ₹2,500 every year (calculated as ₹1,00,000 × 0.025), paying out a total of ₹12,500 in interest alone. If gold prices rise by 20% over that period, the bond's underlying principal value also grows to ₹1,20,000, giving you the benefit of capital appreciation on top of the fixed payout.

In contrast, if you hold ₹1,00,000 in a Gold ETF, the units will mirror the 20% price gain, but you earn no periodic interest, and fund management expenses will slightly lower the net return. If you purchase ₹1,00,000 in jewellery, upfront making charges and taxes mean your money purchases less actual gold, diluting your eventual returns.

Tax Treatment and Holding Costs

Taxes significantly influence your final take-home gains across all three formats.

Physical gold attracts GST on the making charges and purchase value. When sold, the gains are classified as capital gains depending on the holding period.

Gold ETFs incur regular brokerage and demat transaction charges, with annual expense ratios that slightly trim returns over time. When redeemed, the profits are treated under current debt and non-equity mutual fund tax regulations.

SGBs have a distinct structure: the 2.5% annual interest income is taxable as "income from other sources" at your applicable income tax slab rate. On maturity, capital gains from sovereign redemption have historically enjoyed tax relief for individual investors. If sold on the stock exchange before maturity, standard capital gains rules apply.

Because tax laws and classification thresholds evolve with annual union budgets, check the current rules and consult a qualified tax professional before finalizing trades.

Understanding these structural differences ensures you pick the right instrument: physical metal for personal use and wear, Gold ETFs for systematic, low-friction allocations and trading flexibility, or secondary-market SGBs for long-term holding where passive interest income is desired.
